The Saint-Venant equations, one term at a time

Five live numerical experiments. The first switches every source term off and watches the bare hyperbolic core of the equations — a dam break. The next two switch the right-hand side back on, one term at a time: bed slope, then friction. The fourth leaves the line for the plane. The last spins the planet, and the same equations stop describing a flood and start describing a weather front. Every experiment is computed by the same Godunov-type HLL finite-volume scheme, and each is checked against an exact or reference solution, drawn dashed.

With the right-hand side removed, what remains is a pure hyperbolic system with wave speeds u ± √(gh) — formally identical to isentropic gas dynamics with γ = 2, the Froude number playing the role of Mach. Experiment IV keeps the same system but lets the flux point in two directions, x and y. Experiment V adds the third line above and swaps gravity for reduced gravity g′ — at which point the equations describe a layer of cold air rather than a layer of water, and the flood model becomes a weather model.

Experiment I · right-hand side off

The dam break is the Riemann problem

At t = 0 a wall separating two still pools is removed. On a flat, frictionless bed the collapse is exactly solvable — and it contains, in one picture, every wave the shallow-water equations can produce.

Three regions appear instantly and self-similarly — every profile is a stretched copy of the last, a function of x/t alone. Upstream, a rarefaction fan spreads the news of the collapse at speed c₀ = √(gh₀); inside it the surface is a smooth parabola and the characteristics splay apart like spokes. In the middle sits a plateau of constant depth hm, fixed by matching the fan to the shock. Downstream, a bore advances into still water: characteristics converge, the surface steepens into a discontinuity, and mass and momentum are conserved across it while energy is not — the lost energy is real, spent churning the front. Drag the tailwater to zero and the bore disappears entirely: on a dry bed (Ritter, 1892) the solution is all rarefaction, the front runs at 2c₀, and the depth at the dam site locks to exactly 4⁄9 h₀.

Why this problem anchors everything: the numerical scheme is a Godunov method, and at every cell interface, every timestep, it confronts two constant states meeting at a discontinuity — which is precisely a dam break. The HLL flux answers it approximately, bracketing the fan–plateau–shock structure between two estimated wave speeds. The scene you are watching is the solver's own inner subproblem, blown up to the size of the domain. And because the exact answer is known, the readouts above are a genuine validation: a conservative scheme must get the bore speed right, and the visible smearing of the front is the honest signature of first-order numerics.

Experiment II · bed slope on

Flow over a bump, and the art of doing nothing

Now the bed term −gh ∂zb/∂x switches on. Water is forced over a parabolic bump: it accelerates, passes through critical flow at the crest, goes supercritical down the far side, and shocks back to subcritical in a standing hydraulic jump — a steady solution you can derive with Bernoulli and check below.

The subtlety here is not the jump — it is rest. Discretize the flux and the bed term independently and they fail to cancel over a sloping bed, so a perfectly still lake develops phantom currents out of pure truncation error. A scheme that keeps exact stillness is called well-balanced (it satisfies the "C-property"), and the one running above uses hydrostatic reconstruction to achieve it: press lake at rest and watch the maximum velocity readout sit at machine precision — the discrete flux gradient and the discrete bed force cancelling identically, forever. For a flood model this is not a nicety. A river valley is nothing but sloping terrain holding nearly-still water, and a scheme that cannot do nothing cannot be trusted to do something.

The transcritical case adds the second lesson: the flow chooses its own regime. Upstream the flow is subcritical (Fr < 1, information travels both ways), the crest pins Fr = 1 exactly — the shallow-water version of a nozzle throat — and downstream of the jump the flow is subcritical again, which is why fixing the outlet depth is a legitimate boundary condition. The jump itself is a shock that has stopped moving: the same Rankine–Hugoniot conditions as the dam-break bore, with the same energy loss, now anchored in place.

Friction rewrites the front. In the frictionless solution the tip is a wedge tapering to zero depth at 2c₀; with roughness the depth-to-the-¹⁄₃-power in the denominator makes drag explode precisely where the water is thinnest, so the tip blunts into a steep snout and the front falls further and further behind the inviscid prediction — Whitham showed the tip becomes a boundary layer where friction and gravity balance and inertia drops out entirely. Slide n upward and watch the wave change character: by n ≈ 0.16 it is less a wave than a slow spreading, the regime where the kinematic-wave approximation used in simpler flood-routing models becomes honest.

The presets are not generic: they are the NLCD land-cover classes a real flood pipeline maps to Manning coefficients — open water 0.030, grassland 0.035, shrub 0.080, evergreen forest 0.160. In an operational model that coefficient field is the terrain's fingerprint, resampled from satellite land cover onto every grid cell, and this experiment is why it matters: arrival time, the quantity a flood forecast most owes the public, is set largely by this one empirical number.

Experiment IV · two dimensions

The grid is square. The physics is round.

A circular column of water collapses on a plane — the 2-D dam break. The solver knows nothing about circles: it computes 1-D HLL fluxes across the faces of a 168 × 168 Cartesian grid, x-faces and y-faces, exactly as Experiment I did on a line. Whether a round bore emerges from that square machinery is the test.

Two checks run continuously. The cross-section is compared against a reference computed live by a separate 1-D solver in radial coordinates — the same equations with a geometric source term −hu/r, which is why the 2-D bore is weaker than its 1-D cousin: the same water must feed an ever-growing circumference. And the asymmetry readout samples the bore ring at 72 angles: the departure from a perfect circle is the scheme's directional error, the residue of pretending a plane is a stack of rows and columns. Switch the boundaries to walls and the problem leaves textbook territory: the bore reflects, refocuses through the center, and the reference solution goes silent — there is no exact answer anymore, only conservation. Which is the punchline: with closed walls the total volume readout holds to machine precision, because a finite-volume scheme moves water between cells and never invents any.

This experiment is, at small scale, what a 2-D flood model is. Replace the flat floor with a DEM, the uniform roughness with the land-cover Manning field from Experiment III, the circular column with rainfall and river inflows — and the machinery running in this panel, face-normal Riemann problems on a structured grid, is what computes an inundation map. Every term in this page's equations is now switched on somewhere in that computation.

Experiment V · rotation on

Why fronts don't flatten

Same collapse as Experiment I, on a planet that spins — and now the fluid is air: a 1 km layer of cold air, denser than the air above it by about 3%, so gravity is reduced to g′ = gΔθ/θ ≈ 0.3 m/s² and the wave speed drops from 140 m/s to a leisurely 17 m/s. The dense air is 100 m deeper on the left. Released, it should slump and spread until flat, exactly as the dam break did. It doesn't.

Watch the two runs diverge. Without rotation (the dotted ghost) the front collapses into a spreading pair of waves and keeps going — by the end of the run its edges have left the frame, and given enough time the layer would be flat everywhere. With rotation, the slumping air is deflected by Coriolis into an along-front jet, the jet's own deflection opposes further slumping, and within about one inertial period the collapse simply stops. What remains is a permanently tilted interface held up by a wind — the definition of a front. The distance over which it arrests is the Rossby radius of deformation, a = √(g′H)/f: drag the latitude slider and both the jet width and the frontal width follow it, because that single length is what rotation and gravity negotiate.

The dashed curve is not a fit — it is the balanced state predicted before the run starts, from conservation of potential vorticity alone. Each column of air carries its PV, (f + ∂v/∂x)/h, unchanged as it moves; requiring the final state to be both geostrophic and PV-preserving gives a linear equation whose solution the page computes by convolving the initial profile with e−|x|/a. That the simulation walks to that curve on its own is the payoff: the end state was knowable from the start, without solving a single timestep.

Two honest wrinkles, both visible. The jet rings: it overshoots and wobbles around the balanced state at exactly the inertial period 2π/f (16.9 hours at 45°), because adjustment does not gently settle — it dumps the excess energy into inertia-gravity waves that race outward at 17 m/s and leave the domain, and the residue oscillates. Balance is what's left when the waves have gone, which is why forecasters speak of balanced and unbalanced flow as separate worlds. Second, the run uses a sponge layer in the outer quarter of the domain to soak up those departing waves, as operational models do — without it they reflect off the boundary and slosh back through the answer.

This is the experiment that connects the page to weather. The Rossby radius is why atmospheric fronts, jet streams, ocean currents and the filaments of an atmospheric river have the widths they do — a few hundred kilometers, not a few thousand. It also explains why the shallow-water equations remain a working tool in atmospheric science rather than only in hydraulics: with g replaced by g′ and a Coriolis term added, the same five lines of flux and source that routed a flood over a floodplain now hold up a weather front.

Coda

Where Saint-Venant ends

The model assumes hydrostatic pressure — fine while the surface varies slowly, false at the front. Real bores with Froude numbers below about 1.3 do not break; they go undular, radiating a train of smooth waves where dispersion balances steepening — Boussinesq physics, the same family as internal waves in a stratified fluid. And the same mathematics ports across density interfaces: a dam break between two fluids of slightly different density is the lock-exchange gravity current, governed by the two-layer equations with g replaced by g′ = gΔρ/ρ. One wave structure, from flash floods to exchange flows through a strait.

Exact and reference solutions: Ritter (1892), dry-bed dam break; Stoker (1957), wet bed; Goutal & Maurel (1997) bump test cases via steady Bernoulli theory; 1-D radial shallow-water equations for the circular case; linear Rossby adjustment via potential-vorticity conservation (Rossby, 1938; Gill, 1982, §7.2) for the rotating case. Scheme: first-order Godunov finite volume, HLL interface fluxes (Toro, 2001), hydrostatic reconstruction for well-balancing (Audusse et al., 2004), semi-implicit Manning friction, exact rotation for Coriolis, CFL 0.9 (1-D) / 0.45 (2-D).
